## This hunt started at a printer

Five years before I pulled the trigger, this hunt started with me hunched over a printer, feeding it paper.

I didn't want to pay a print shop to blow up a giant map of the country I wanted to learn, so I found a way to chop a topo map into printer-paper-sized chunks and printed them one at a time. Thirty sheets. I taped them all together into one huge map, spread it out, and went to work with a highlighter: every single road, marked. Because once every road glows yellow, the places *without* any yellow jump off the paper — the roadless pockets where people aren't walking around. I found four of them worth a look.

I'd also paid for an e-scouting class around then — how to read glassing angles, why you plan your sits looking into north-facing slopes, how to map your miles and budget a day. Homework on top of homework, for a chunk of Montana public land I'm not going to name any closer than that.

Then life did what life does. For four years, that map stayed folded.

## The trade I'd make again

This fall I missed most of archery season because I got married in September — right in the middle of it, with a brand-new bow at home and my first archery bull still unchecked on the list. I'm not complaining. I married a woman who hunts, which means I didn't lose a season. I gained a hunting partner.

So we pulled out the old map and went to look at two of the four circles.

## Weekend one: fog, and my wife outshoots me

The weekend was socked in — fog so thick the higher spot never opened up all day. We dropped to the lower circle and started hiking, and on our very first walk in, my wife killed her buck. A hundred and fifteen yards, and she didn't just hit it — she split the heart in half. He stumbled, tipped over backwards, done.

Then came the part nobody puts on camera: a two-mile drag in the dark. We tried every method known to man — back legs, antlers, rope around my waist with her steering the legs through the brush — rotating through them as each one wore us out. She's tougher than most men I know. And when it came time to gut it, she waved me off: "I'm doing my own deer." It had been years for her, and she wanted it back. She worked like a surgeon — never popped a thing, kept every ounce of meat clean. We spread it open to cool overnight and camped next to a bull elk that bellowed and crashed around half the night, dying to know what was going on in our camp.

## Sixteen does and a fire argument

Sunday we hiked a loop for my buck and bumped eight does that moseyed off without real alarm. That got my heart going — everything I've read says the big buck beds where the does are and won't show himself until he thinks he can't be seen.

My wife was freezing and the whole mountain was that not-raining-but-everything's-damp kind of wet, so I found a deep little draw and started building her a fire. She was *not* on board — "is this even legal? We're going to light this whole mountainside on fire." I built a ring, told her to trust me, and got her warm. (She forgave me somewhere around the second hour of being not-cold.)

Then I went back to glassing and found eight more does across the ravine. Sixteen total, parked in one deep, twisting cut — and I'd have bet anything there was a buck hiding in there with them. We glassed until we ran out of Sunday, and here's my honest gear confession: I run low-end binoculars, and my rifle and scope *together* cost about five hundred bucks. The scope gives me less detail than the binos. I strained at that ravine for hours and couldn't pull a buck out of it. We hiked out, found six more does on the way, and I wrote the spot down in ink.

## The midweek window

The next weekend was elk camp — our party took two bulls and a nice buck; my wife and I blanked. But my old hunting partner was taking the following week off, headed to the same general forest chasing whitetail, and I'm a general contractor: when a job finishes early, the mountains call. I wrapped the job Tuesday and rolled in.

Driving the bottom country on day one, I glassed up a genuinely nice 3x4 — wide past his ears, tall, stiff-legging around after does — and got good video of him. First deer of the trip and the cameras were already earning their pack weight. My buddy struck out on whitetail after two days and texted: *I'm coming to hunt with you.* I sent him a pin and showed him the video. His review: "that's one of the nicer ones I've seen."

## Coffee at one in the morning

I couldn't sleep. Up at 1 a.m. thinking about work, so I leaned into it — sent my emails, sorted my pack, and brewed coffee strong, cut with boiling water, into a thermos. That's the trick: smoking-hot coffee at the glassing point, hours later, when it matters.

Walking at five. Glassing spot by six, half an hour before shooting light — and for once the spot I'd picked off the map was *exactly* as advertised. Perfect seat, perfect angles. We earned a slow day for it: one forkhorn spent the whole morning harassing a doe, napping, and harassing her again. My partner snuck a look at the far side that evening — three bucks, a handful of does, nothing worth burning a tag on.

That night we made the call: back to the doe ravine.

## Sentry does

Somebody had beaten us to the way in I'd used before — so we adjusted, and figured if he bumped anything, he'd push it straight to us. We climbed. A thousand feet of elevation, stopping to glass at every excuse, staying just under the skyline.

Thirteen does. Most tucked out of the wind in a protected pocket — but two of them stationed high on the edge, heads on a swivel, watching the old road below. Sentries. I'd never watched it that clearly before: the herd posts guards, and the guards watch the ways in.

They were 1,100 to 1,500 yards out with no hidden path to them. So we dropped off the ridge and went the long way — sidehilling around cliffs, bear-crawling the exposed stretches. It had been 28 degrees at dawn; by now it was 65, and we were cooking inside our layers. We stopped, opened everything up, dried out. I picked sage and crushed it into my pockets and over my clothes — my homemade scent cover, free on every hillside.

The plan was patient: get above the does, downwind, boots off, cook lunch, and wait for the rut to deliver a buck to them. My map said we were still 700 yards out. I was creeping anyway — slower with every step, short quiet moves, keeping pace with my partner so nobody felt rushed.

And all of a sudden, there's a buck *right there.*

## The standoff

A hundred and fifty yards. Bedded in the steepest, deepest draw on that mountain, with a back-door exit he could vanish through in two steps. Locked onto us, stone still.

I squatted down and put the rifle on him, and then began the strangest 45 minutes of my hunting life. He never stood. He barely moved his head. The big ones don't flush, I'd always heard — they hold like a rooster pheasant and let you walk by. So we settled into a siege: me glued to the scope, my buddy easing my camera and tripod out of my pack while I taught him the buttons in whispers — *that one. No, that one. Focus ring. Hit record.* When I had to look away, he kept eyes on the buck; when he had a question, I'd glance over, point, and get back on the scope.

Here's where the cheap glass almost cost me the biggest buck of my life: at 150 yards, I could not tell what he was. Tall, yes — taller than anything I'd ever shot. Heavy, clearly. Right about at his ear tips. I even pulled up the photo of my wife's buck on my phone to compare, like I was shopping. Finally a small head-tilt showed us the fourth point, and I made the call I'd been circling for most of an hour: a tall, heavy public-land 4x4, on camera? I guess I do want this buck.

## The shot I'd never practiced

Nobody teaches you the bedded shot. He was ever-so-slightly quartered toward me, shoulder tucked up and shielding his chest, with a little mound of dirt in front of him stealing the low line. So I built the shot like a puzzle: just in front of the shoulder, into the "collarbone" — as low as the mound allowed, angling for the vitals, off my knee, shooting uphill, both cameras rolling — one on me, one on him.

I squeezed. He dropped like a sack of potatoes — never twitched, never kicked. High-shoulder, done where he lay, the way sheep hunters shoot so nothing tumbles off a cliff. We sat for a minute, let the steam blow off, and shook hands on the stalk.

## Ground growth

The walk up that draw was the best kind of brutal — straight up the bottom, because the sides were too steep to sidehill. And the closer we got, the bigger he grew. He'd hidden it well, bedded: this buck is a heavy, dark 4x4 with character on *every* point. Main beams hooking hard at the tips. Eye guards hooking forward. Points curling inward. Symmetric all the way through. We just stood there, baffled and grinning.

Then the details that remind you public-land deer live a hard life: a coiled spring cinched around one ankle — scars and calluses underneath it, a rock jammed in the coils, something he'd been hauling around for a long, long time. And a colony of ticks nested inside one ear, which I removed from the situation at maximum range. This buck had earned easier years than the ones he got.

## Packing out a prayer

We split him up: my buddy took a hindquarter and a front shoulder for me; I packed the other quarter and shoulder, both backstraps, the tenderloins, and the skull lashed to the outside of my pack. A mile and a half out, heavy as it should be.

Halfway through loading up, my buddy said we ought to celebrate somehow, and apologized that we hadn't packed anything to do it with. I told him honestly: "I'm just going to celebrate by saying a prayer." He thought about it and said, "That's a good answer."

So that's what I did, walking out under the weight — no kneeling, no ceremony, just talking to Him like He was walking beside me. Thank you. I put in the work, I prayed for this for years, and You put him right in front of me, on a perfect day, next to an old friend.

## The calls home

Back at camp I've got a satellite connection, so I called my wife: "I'm sending you a picture." *"Did you get one?"* "You're going to have to wait and see." The photo landed and the phone nearly exploded — *"Is that YOURS? No way. That's a NICE buck, babe!"* — equal parts celebration and heartbreak, because she'd wanted to be there for the pack-out so badly. She also immediately claimed the announcement rights: "Don't you post it. I want to write something for you."

Then I texted her dad. He called before the picture finished downloading. The weekend before, at elk camp, he'd taught me his trophy-photo trick — tilt the head forward; it opens the frame up and shows the buck honestly big — and now he was on the phone saying *I'm so proud of you* in a way that choked me up more than I'll usually admit. For a guy who doesn't do giddy, I did a passable impression of it that day.

## What actually killed this buck

Not the rifle — though the $500 combo did everything I asked at 150 yards. Not the optics — those nearly talked me *out* of him. What killed this buck was five years old and made of printer paper:

- **The map.** Thirty taped pages and a highlighter. Mark every road; hunt where the yellow isn't.
- **The homework.** The e-scouting class taught me to pick glassing seats off the topo — and the seat was perfect when I finally sat in it.
- **Does first, bucks second.** Twenty-two does on one weekend wrote this story's ending two weeks early. In the rut, the does are the X on the map.
- **Respect the sentries.** The herd posts guards and the guards watch the trails in. Go the long, ugly, bear-crawl way around.
- **Big bucks hold.** He let us walk to 150 and never stood. If I'd been moving fast, he'd have let us walk right on by.

The hunt itself felt almost easy — one climb, one stalk, one shot. But easy is what five years of work looks like when it finally cashes out.

And the first gear upgrade off the back of this hunt? Binoculars. Because I never want to spend 45 minutes unable to tell whether the biggest buck of my life is the biggest buck of my life. When I upgrade, you'll get the full field test here — real miles, real verdicts, same as always.
